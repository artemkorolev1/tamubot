# 1.4 Course Description


Management Control Systems (MCSs) are a formal competency area with the Engineering Management body of knowledge.  It is essential for engineering managers to understand the nature of MCSs, their design, and their remediation.  The foundation for MCSs is systems science.  Additionally, managerial cybernetics and systems engineering play a major role in the design, implementation, and operation of MCSs.

MCSs are 'defined broadly to include everything managers do to help ensure that an organization's strategies and plans are carried out or, if conditions warrant, are modified.' For a given MCS the result is often a fusion of action-based, results-based, and personnel/cultural-based controls.  This course

examines what these controls are and how they exert their influence in the organization.  Further, we examine and exercise methods for MCS analysis and design.

The object, the *thing* to be controlled, is broadly identified as the purposeful organization.  Purposeful in that the intention of the organization is to remain economically viable.  The purposeful organization is people centric.  It is the behavior of the people that comprise the organization that is the focus of the management controls.  Systems engineering provides a set of methods, tools, and techniques to represent the organization to facilitate the integration of the various controls into the operation of the organization.  Unlike the object of most engineering design efforts, the purposeful organization, as the object of our design, has no physical analog!  Thus, we leverage various system engineering methods and models in order to develop representations of the organization that allow us to both analyze and design the form, fit, function, and controls of the organization.  The foundational theorem for organization design and control is the Conant-Ashby theorem:  "Every good regulator of a system is a model of the system that it regulates."

## 1.10 Course Synopsis and Topical Schedule

I will provide a more detailed description of the material for each week/class in the lecture notes. MCS is a blend of principle and practice applied to production/productive systems.  A production system is one that generates valued goods and services -the PS constitutes a type of purposeful organization. The production system itself is an engineered integration of people, parts, processes, facilities, equipment, policies, etc.  For the engineering systems manager the PS itself is subject to the engineering life-cycle.  The focus of the MCS is on the PS, and more specifically the behavior of the people in the organization.  Systems engineering provides a compendium of methods and models to represent and characterize the complex nature of systems and their interdependencies.  The course is architected to expose the students to the knowledge, skills, and experiences (KSEs) of organizational systems.  The semester project provides the experiential component, the text and state of the practice readings provide the knowledge, and classroom exercises, management tools, homework, and quizzes provide the backbone to assess the skills dimension.
ISEN 663, while not requiring either ISEN 640 or ISEN 665 as pre or co-requisites -overlaps with both. ISEN 665 is focused on the core functions and skills of the engineering manager; ISEN 640 is focused on providing the ISEN with the *systems thinking* lens -for holistic considerations when dealing with large complex systems.  We initiate our discussion of organizational systems with the basic types of organizations and their core elements (using the classic dimensions of form, fit, and function).  We then examine several methods, prominent in industry, for characterizing organizations -both for design and analysis.  Next, we focus on the *control* facet of organizations -those managerial constructs that are put in place to ensure behaviors are congruent with the mission, vision, and values of the purposeful organization.  Finally, we examine the organizational roles and responsibilities in the effective management of the large complex engineering system.  Readings will be identified in the lecture notes. The socio-technical nature of the organizations, endemic to ISEN, necessitates study of both the behavior of individuals and groups/teams.
As is the norm with SEbased courses, ISEN 663 relies on several 'reference models' or REFMODs. These are the bread and butter of the engineering consultant and the engineering manager; more to say on that point as we progress.  REFMODs embody the principles and best practices of ORG SYS analysis

and design.  MCS -as an academic discipline -depends heavily on REFMODs.  With this in mind, the course is divided into two major thrusts: the first is focused on the common REFMODs used to represent the purposeful organization.  The need is clear, we need ways of representing the *thing* that we are designing or remediating.  Unlike tangible parts, products, buildings, etc. -organizations defy physical representation.  Logical equivalents are the norm -and the number of logical equivalents used in industry for this purpose is manifold.  ISEN 663 will focus on the most commonly used REFMODs.  The second core thrust of ISEN 663 is focused on the concept of control.  Since the organizations that we are analyzing and defining are human-centric -it is the behavior of the constituencies within the ORG that we are attempting to control.  To have a control is to make comparisons.  Management controls are established to ensure the behaviors of the firm's employees are congruent with the firm's mission, vision, values, value propositions, supporting functions, etc.  The management control system then is the entire set of management controls requisite to achieve this congruence.

## 1.12 Leveraging AI technology as a resource

The ability to rapidly assimilate information across a large number of disparate sources is very useful to the engineer.  Unfortunately, the use and acceptance of AI technology is still in its infancy and the veracity of the AI generated information is not easily vetted nor is there a standard process by which the vetting is acceptable across the academic community.  Regardless, it is a resource -but due to its volatility (same prompt, different AI-bot, varied response) and sometimes questionable provenance (what, exactly, did it assimilate in order to generate its response) the use of this technology must be carefully documented by you.  The burden of veracity is placed on the user.
If you choose to leverage AI technologies of any type then you must, append to your submission, the following documentation:
4. A discussion of how and where you leveraged the AI generated material in the course artifact being produced
5. A full verification, by you, of the response from the AI-bot; this is akin to a literature review for each of the points made by the AI-bot that were leveraged by you in your submission
6. A statement, signed by you, attesting to the veracity of the response from the AI-bot
    Clearly, with a timed quiz, use of AI technology makes little sense -since you would have the added burden of supplying the aforementioned documentation as part of the timed submission.  Additionally, the HW, midterm, and project are largely model-based, thus the use of AI may also be of limited value versus the additional documentation required.
