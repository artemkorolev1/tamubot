### 2.1 CATALOG DESCRIPTION

Introduction to the systems thinking process and the fundamental considerations associated with the engineering of large-scale systems, or systems engineering.

## 2.5 EXPECTATIONS
This is an introductory course on Systems Engineering and students will learn how to think in terms of engineered systems and the characteristics of their life cycle.  They will learn how to critically think about and analyze symptoms, concerns, and opportunities and from those identify or diagnose the root causes that must be addressed by the system.  They will learn to formulate requirements that are testable and achievable.  They will learn basic techniques for system design, model-based analysis, and documentation of requirements and designs in formal languages (SySML and UML) They will learn the basics of organizing and leading a system development effort.  In addition to the classroom instruction will also be 'hands' experience applying these techniques on a realistic system challenge. Students will work on an individual or team semester homework involving a challenging real-life societal problem. The student will apply the systems engineering approach to model, analyze and design a system solution to address the challenge. With instructor approval, a student can opt to work on a challenge related to their line of work, thesis or dissertation. In this case, the student should send a request to the instructor for approval. Student teams will be organized and each team will set up their own periodic meetings via Zoom (e.g., weekly).

#### Online Course Etiquette 1
1. BE ON TIME, PRESENT, AND ENGAGED DURING THE ENTIRE LECTURE.
We will have pop up questions and quizzes from time to time, as well as frequent breakout rooms. Your absence will make the experience worse for you and everyone else.
2. USE YOUR REAL NAME.
Make sure your Zoom name is set to be your real name. Your participation grade will be based on this. Additionally, if the instructor/TA/graders do not recognize your name, then you may be booted out.
3. MUTE YOUR MICROPHONE.
Please keep your microphone muted if you are not in a breakout room or actively speaking to the class. There are lots of outdoor sounds that multiply with many students. Please make the listening experience better for everyone by muting your microphone when you are not using it.
4. KEEP YOUR VIDEO ON.
By seeing your video, it helps to see if you are engaged and allows me to adapt the material appropriately. An enormous amount of feedback comes from your faces. I am showing my face, please show me yours. If this expectation causes a problem for a student, e.g., lack of webcam or bandwidth issues, an exception will be made. We expect students to remember and follow the Aggie Honor Code when requesting an exception.

12. LET ME KNOW IF YOU NEED SPECIAL ACCOMMODATIONS.
If you have an accommodations letter from Disability Services, please contact me so we can make sure the course organization works for you. I will not realize that something is wrong if you do not tell me.
13. USE AFTER CLASS SURVEY TO LIST ANY CONFUSIONS THAT REMAIN.
After the Zoom is completed, you will be shown a thumbs-up or thumbs-down. If you select thumbs-down, you are provided a text box. Feel free to use this to specify any confusions that you still have about the course content.
14. RESPECT OTHERS AND THEIR OPINIONS.
In online learning, students from very different backgrounds come together to learn. It is important to respect their feelings and opinions even when they are quite different from your own.
15. WATCH YOUR LANGUAGE AND TONE CAREFULLY.
When you are communicating online, you have to rely solely on words to get your point across. The other person cannot see your facial expression or hear your tone of voice, so things like sarcasm and humor often do not come across very well. Tha t's why it's important to take your time, choose your words carefully, and be as straightforward as you can.
16. CONSIDER PEOPLE ' S PRIVACY.
Always ask for permission before you forward someone's email messages to somebody else, and if you do reuse somebody els e's words (with their permission), make sure to acknowledge them appropriately. Keep in mind that all private email is considered copyrighted by the original author.
17. AVOID INAPPROPRIATE MATERIAL.
It is tempting to forward messages you find clever or links to websites you find entertaining to classmates. If they are not directly relevant to the course, please do not do this, especially if the material is sexually suggestive, politically sensitive, or otherwise 'edgy.' The same goes for frivolous 'joke' emai ls and chain messages. This is not the place for it.
18. BE FORGIVING.
If someone writes something that you find offensive, mention it directly to the instructor. He or she is best equipped to address the situation. Remember that the person contributing to the discussion might be new to this form of communication. What you find offensive may have been unintended.
19. BE CONCISE.
When you are contributing to a discussion, be as brief as you can to get your point across. Adding a lot of unnecessary words just makes your message less clear. Try to stick to the point and not go off on irrelevant tangents.
20. READ FIRST, WRITE LATER.
Don't add your comments to a discussion before reading the comments that are already there. And if you are responding to a previous comment, always make clear which comment you are responding to.
21. DON'T TYPE IN ALL CAPS OR USE EXCESSIVE PUNCTUATION!!!!!!!
Most people find this annoying and you may not be communicating your thoughts effectively.
22. THINK BEFORE YOU HIT THE SEND BUTTON.
Learning to be your own editor is a difficult and important skill. Think carefully about the content of your message before you send or post it. Once you push the button, there is no taking it back. Grammar and spelling errors reflect badly on you, and misspelled words or poorly constructed sentences can make it hard to decipher your meaning accurately.
